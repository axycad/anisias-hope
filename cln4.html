CLN4 (Batten disease / NCL)

CLN4 disease is an adult onset form of Batten disease caused by changes in the DNAJC5 gene. Unlike the other forms, one changed copy of the gene is enough to cause it.

Family guide Last reviewed: 3 November 2026

Overview

CLN4 disease is caused by changes in the DNAJC5 gene, which makes a protein called cysteine string protein alpha (CSPα). In the brain this protein helps nerve cells pass signals to each other by keeping other proteins correctly folded.

It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern: one changed copy of the gene, from either parent, is enough to cause the condition. It is the only form of NCL known to be inherited this way. The adult forms of NCL, including CLN4, are sometimes called Kufs disease.

Signs and typical age of onset

Symptoms typically appear around age 30, but can begin at any time from the teenage years to late adulthood. They include seizures with muscle jerks (myoclonic epilepsy), a decline in memory and thinking (dementia), problems with balance and coordination, tremors and speech difficulties. Orphanet notes that sight loss is usually absent.

Every person is different, and the pattern described in sources is a guide only.

How it is diagnosed in the UK

If a doctor suspects Batten disease, the person is referred to specialists, usually in neurology or metabolic medicine. The specialist team decides which tests are right.

In England, the NHS National Genomic Test Directory includes test R231, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis. It is a gene panel (Genomics England PanelApp panel 526) that includes DNAJC5. Clinical genetics, metabolic medicine or neurology teams can request it when there are signs typical of NCL, such as vacuolated lymphocytes on a blood film, storage material on a tissue biopsy, or low enzyme activity. Broader genetic testing for inherited metabolic conditions (R98) may also find it (NHS England test directories; PanelApp 526).

In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, genetic testing is arranged through each nation's own NHS genetics service.

Treatment and care in the UK

There is no licensed medicine that slows or stops CLN4 disease. As at 3 November 2026, the only medicine authorised by the European Medicines Agency for any form of NCL is Brineura, which is for CLN2 only (EMA).

Care focuses on symptoms and quality of life, for example seizures, movement, feeding, sight and communication. It is led by the specialist team, working with local NHS services.

Where UK families can get help

Your clinical team comes first for diagnosis, care and any questions about studies. These services can also help:

  • For adults, ask your neurologist or GP about referral to a specialist adult neurology or metabolic service. The children's services below may still be able to advise your team.
  • Batten Disease Family Association (BDFA): the UK charity for families affected by Batten disease, now registered as the Batten Disease Family Association CIO, charity 1205650.
  • Evelina London Batten disease service: a specialist service at St Thomas' Hospital, London, which takes referrals on a shared care basis so that local teams keep caring for a child with expert input.
  • Great Ormond Street Hospital, metabolic medicine: GOSH's enzyme laboratory also offers NHS blood tests for the CLN1 and CLN2 enzymes.
  • NIHR Be Part of Research: the National Institute for Health and Care Research service where you can search UK studies and register your interest.
  • If you are worried about symptoms now, contact your GP or NHS 111.
  • Newly diagnosed and FAQ: other organisations and public information
  • Contact Anisia's Hope at info@fightingbattendisease.org
