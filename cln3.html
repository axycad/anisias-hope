CLN3 (Batten disease / NCL)

CLN3 disease, sometimes called juvenile Batten disease, is caused by changes in the CLN3 gene. Sight problems often begin between the ages of 4 and 8.

Family guide Last reviewed: 3 November 2026

Overview

CLN3 disease is caused by changes in the CLN3 gene, which makes a protein sometimes called battenin. It sits in the membranes of lysosomes and related parts of the cell, and its exact job is still not fully understood. Orphanet describes CLN3 as the most common form of NCL.

It is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. A child is affected when both copies of the gene, one from each parent, carry a change. Parents who each carry one changed copy usually have no symptoms themselves.

Signs and typical age of onset

After several years of typical development, sight problems often begin between ages 4 and 8, caused by breakdown of the retina at the back of the eye. Around the same age, children may fall behind at school and begin to lose skills, often starting with speech.

Seizures and movement problems, such as stiffness, slowness and a stooped posture, often develop in the teenage years. Some people develop an irregular heart rhythm later on, and a small number have hallucinations. Most people with CLN3 disease live into early adulthood.

Every person is different, and the pattern described in sources is a guide only.

How it is diagnosed in the UK

If a doctor suspects Batten disease, the person is referred to specialists, usually in neurology or metabolic medicine. The specialist team decides which tests are right.

In England, the NHS National Genomic Test Directory includes test R231, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis. It is a gene panel (Genomics England PanelApp panel 526) that includes CLN3. Clinical genetics, metabolic medicine or neurology teams can request it when there are signs typical of NCL, such as vacuolated lymphocytes on a blood film, storage material on a tissue biopsy, or low enzyme activity. Broader genetic testing for inherited metabolic conditions (R98) may also find it (NHS England test directories; PanelApp 526).

In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, genetic testing is arranged through each nation's own NHS genetics service.

Treatment and care in the UK

There is no licensed medicine that slows or stops CLN3 disease. As at 3 November 2026, the only medicine authorised by the European Medicines Agency for any form of NCL is Brineura, which is for CLN2 only (EMA). A 2025 study in Neurology also states that no disease modifying treatments are available (PMID 40924969).

Care focuses on symptoms and quality of life, for example seizures, movement, feeding, sight and communication. It is led by the specialist team, working with local NHS services.

Open trials for CLN3

16 registered studies worldwide include CLN3, of which 4 are recruiting (registries checked 3 November 2026). The trials page gives registry numbers, sponsors, countries and status.

Research and publications

  • A small open label study of miglustat in six people with CLN3 was published in Neurology in 2025 (PMID 40924969). Miglustat was well tolerated, and the authors report a slower rate of physical decline than in historical comparison data. They note the limits: six people, no control group, and everyone knew the treatment given.

Research pipeline

Programmes not yet in a registered trial, from company, regulator and research funder announcements. We state only what each programme is and its stage, not claims about results. Laboratory and preclinical work may take many years to reach people, and many programmes stop.

  • Exon skipping antisense medicine. Rosalind Franklin University, with Ionis Pharmaceuticals and Sanford Research, US. Approach: Antisense oligonucleotide for the common CLN3 deletion. Stage: Preclinical (mouse and cell studies). Source: Nature Medicine, 2020, PMID 32719489.
  • Zebronkysen. UNC Health, US, with the ForeBatten Foundation. Approach: Personalised antisense medicine given into the spinal fluid. Stage: Two children treated in an N of 2 study under a US FDA authorised protocol since June 2024; no registry entry found. Source: UNC Health news, September 2024.
  • Comparing treatments in a patient derived retina model. University of Rochester, US. Approach: Recombinant human acid ceramidase compared with miglustat and others, in retina cells grown from patient stem cells. Stage: Preclinical. Source: BDGRI 2026 grants.
  • Causes of swallowing difficulty. Washington University in St Louis, US. Approach: Laboratory research in mouse models of CLN1, CLN2 and CLN3. Stage: Laboratory research. Source: BDGRI 2025 grants.
  • Biomarkers in body fluids. Sanford Research, University of South Dakota, US. Approach: Measures of disease in large animal models of CLN1, CLN2, CLN3, CLN5 and CLN6. Stage: Laboratory research. Source: BDGRI 2025 grants.

The CLN3 gene therapy CLN-301 (formerly AT-GTX-502) remains in a registered phase 1/2 study, listed on the trials page. Amicus Therapeutics described it as ongoing in early 2022 (Amicus annual report, SEC); the registry now lists Neela Therapeutics as sponsor.

Registered studies, including those that have finished, are on the CLN3 trials page. BDGRI is the Batten Disease Global Research Initiative, a group of patient charities that funds research together.

Where UK families can get help

Your clinical team comes first for diagnosis, care and any questions about studies. These services can also help:

  • Batten Disease Family Association (BDFA): the UK charity for families affected by Batten disease, now registered as the Batten Disease Family Association CIO, charity 1205650.
  • Evelina London Batten disease service: a specialist service at St Thomas' Hospital, London, which takes referrals on a shared care basis so that local teams keep caring for a child with expert input.
  • Great Ormond Street Hospital, metabolic medicine: GOSH's enzyme laboratory also offers NHS blood tests for the CLN1 and CLN2 enzymes.
  • NIHR Be Part of Research: the National Institute for Health and Care Research service where you can search UK studies and register your interest.
  • If you are worried about symptoms now, contact your GP or NHS 111.
  • Newly diagnosed and FAQ: other organisations and public information
  • Contact Anisia's Hope at info@fightingbattendisease.org
