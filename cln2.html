CLN2 (Batten disease / NCL)

CLN2 disease is a form of Batten disease caused by changes in the TPP1 gene. It usually begins between the ages of 2 and 4. It is the only form with a licensed medicine, Brineura.

Family guide Last reviewed: 3 November 2026

Overview

CLN2 disease is caused by changes in the TPP1 gene, which holds the instructions for an enzyme called tripeptidyl peptidase 1. The enzyme works inside lysosomes, the parts of the cell that break down and recycle material. Without enough working enzyme, material builds up in cells and nerve cells in the brain are lost over time.

It is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. A child is affected when both copies of the gene, one from each parent, carry a change. Parents who each carry one changed copy usually have no symptoms themselves.

Signs and typical age of onset

Signs usually begin between ages 2 and 4. The first are often seizures and difficulty coordinating movement (ataxia). Children also develop muscle jerks (myoclonus) and loss of sight, and lose skills they had gained, including walking and speech.

Some children do not show symptoms until after age 4. Their features tend to be milder overall, although problems with coordination can be more marked.

Every person is different, and the pattern described in sources is a guide only.

How it is diagnosed in the UK

If a doctor suspects Batten disease, the person is referred to specialists, usually in neurology or metabolic medicine. The specialist team decides which tests are right.

In England, the NHS National Genomic Test Directory includes test R231, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis. It is a gene panel (Genomics England PanelApp panel 526) that includes TPP1. Clinical genetics, metabolic medicine or neurology teams can request it when there are signs typical of NCL, such as vacuolated lymphocytes on a blood film, storage material on a tissue biopsy, or low enzyme activity. Broader genetic testing for inherited metabolic conditions (R98) may also find it (NHS England test directories; PanelApp 526).

In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, genetic testing is arranged through each nation's own NHS genetics service.

For CLN2, the TPP1 enzyme can be measured in a blood sample at the Great Ormond Street Hospital enzyme laboratory, with a listed turnaround of 4 to 6 weeks; samples taken in pregnancy can also be tested after discussion with the laboratory (GOSH, TPP test). The test directory also has a separate single gene test for CLN2, R271, used where the signs point specifically to CLN2.

Treatment and care in the UK

Brineura (cerliponase alfa) is an enzyme replacement therapy. It replaces the missing TPP1 enzyme and is given every two weeks into the fluid around the brain, through a small device fitted by surgery (EMA). NICE notes that the clinical trial evidence shows it slows the progression of CLN2 (NICE HST34; NEJM, 2018). It is not a cure. Whether a child can start it on the NHS now depends on where in the UK they live.

England

NICE's final guidance HST34 (18 February 2026) does not recommend Brineura for routine NHS use, because it was not judged cost effective. Children already having it, or who started before the managed access period ended, can continue until they and their clinician decide to stop (NICE HST34). Appeals by the company and by the BDFA were dismissed (NICE appeal).

Scotland

The Scottish Medicines Consortium says that from 2 November 2023 Brineura can be prescribed within its ultra orphan pathway while more evidence is gathered, before a decision on routine use in NHSScotland (SMC2286).

Wales

NHS Wales follows NICE here. The All Wales Therapeutics and Toxicology Centre lists Brineura as excluded from Welsh appraisal because of NICE HST34 (AWTTC).

Northern Ireland

The Department of Health reviews NICE guidance and can endorse it. We found no separate Northern Ireland decision on Brineura. Where a medicine is not routinely available, a clinician can make an Individual Funding Request (nidirect).

Alongside or without Brineura, care is supportive. NICE describes standard care as aiming to relieve symptoms and maintain function and quality of life (NICE HST34). This is led by your child's specialist team.

27 registered studies worldwide include CLN2, of which 5 are recruiting (registries checked 3 November 2026). The trials page gives registry numbers, sponsors, countries and status.

Research and publications

  • The main Brineura study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2018, gave the enzyme every two weeks to 24 children aged 3 to 16 and compared their motor and language scores with 42 untreated children from historical records. Treated children lost these skills more slowly. The study was open label, with no placebo group (PMID 29688815).

Research pipeline

Programmes not yet in a registered trial, from company, regulator and research funder announcements. We state only what each programme is and its stage, not claims about results. Laboratory and preclinical work may take many years to reach people, and many programmes stop.

  • LTS-101. Latus Bio. Approach: Gene therapy (AAV). Stage: IND cleared by the US FDA, 2 December 2025; no registry entry found. Source: Latus Bio news.
  • TTX-181 (formerly RGX-181). Tern Therapeutics (originated by REGENXBIO). Approach: Gene therapy (AAV9) delivered to the spinal fluid. Stage: One child treated in an investigator led study in Brazil; Tern said in February 2025 that the US FDA had cleared an IND to begin clinical trials; no registered trial found. REGENXBIO reported the single patient study in 2023 and said it would seek partners for the programme (REGENXBIO quarterly report, SEC, November 2023). Source: Tern Therapeutics, 10 February 2025.
  • JUST-AAV-TPP1. JCR Pharmaceuticals, Japan. Approach: Gene therapy given into the bloodstream using an engineered vector designed to reach the brain. Stage: Preclinical (mouse studies). Source: JCR Pharmaceuticals, May 2026.
  • Gene editing to correct TPP1. Menzies Institute for Medical Research, University of Tasmania, Australia. Approach: Base editing and integrase approaches delivered by AAV. Stage: Preclinical. Source: BDGRI 2026 grants.
  • Newborn screening test for CLN2. UCL Great Ormond Street Institute of Child Health, UK. Approach: Dried blood spot test for TPP1 enzyme activity, to assess feasibility for UK newborn screening. Stage: Diagnostic research. Source: BDGRI 2025 grants.
  • Causes of swallowing difficulty. Washington University in St Louis, US. Approach: Laboratory research in mouse models of CLN1, CLN2 and CLN3. Stage: Laboratory research. Source: BDGRI 2025 grants.
  • Biomarkers in body fluids. Sanford Research, University of South Dakota, US. Approach: Measures of disease in large animal models of CLN1, CLN2, CLN3, CLN5 and CLN6. Stage: Laboratory research. Source: BDGRI 2025 grants.

Registered studies, including those that have finished, are on the CLN2 trials page. BDGRI is the Batten Disease Global Research Initiative, a group of patient charities that funds research together.

Where UK families can get help

Your clinical team comes first for diagnosis, care and any questions about studies. These services can also help:

  • Batten Disease Family Association (BDFA): the UK charity for families affected by Batten disease, now registered as the Batten Disease Family Association CIO, charity 1205650.
  • Evelina London Batten disease service: a specialist service at St Thomas' Hospital, London, which takes referrals on a shared care basis so that local teams keep caring for a child with expert input.
  • Great Ormond Street Hospital, metabolic medicine: GOSH's enzyme laboratory also offers NHS blood tests for the CLN1 and CLN2 enzymes.
  • NIHR Be Part of Research: the National Institute for Health and Care Research service where you can search UK studies and register your interest.
  • If you are worried about symptoms now, contact your GP or NHS 111.
  • Newly diagnosed and FAQ: other organisations and public information
  • Contact Anisia's Hope at info@fightingbattendisease.org
