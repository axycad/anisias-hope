CLN9 (Batten disease / NCL)

CLN9 is a historical label. It is no longer listed as a separate form of Batten disease, and no CLN9 gene has been identified.

Family guide Last reviewed: 3 November 2026

Overview

CLN9 was a name given in the early 2000s to a small number of families whose condition looked like Batten disease but did not match the forms known at the time. Later genetic testing found changes in known genes in at least some of these families. It is no longer used as a separate type, and no CLN9 gene has been identified. Orphanet now marks CLN9 disease as obsolete (Orphanet).

This is why the CLN numbers on our site run from 1 to 14 with a gap at 9. If you have seen the label in older letters or articles, your clinical genetics team can explain what it means for your family.

How it is diagnosed in the UK

There is no CLN9 test, because no CLN9 gene has been identified. The NHS gene panel for NCL in England (Genomics England PanelApp panel 526) does not include a CLN9 gene (PanelApp 526). If your family was given this label in the past, a clinical genetics team can say whether newer testing would help.

Treatment and care in the UK

Treatment and care depend on the confirmed diagnosis. If a family once told "CLN9" now has a CLN5 or CLN2 diagnosis, the pages for those forms apply: CLN5 and CLN2.

Open trials for CLN9

There are no trials for CLN9, because it is no longer a separate condition. The trials page explains where to look instead.

Research and publications

  • A 2025 review of NCL classification explains how the CLN9 families were reclassified (PMID 39925015).

Research pipeline

None. CLN9 is no longer a separate condition, so no programme targets it.

Historic notes

CLN9 was proposed in the early 2000s for a small number of families whose cells behaved differently from known forms (for example Annals of Neurology, 2004). Later genetic work found changes in known genes: a 2025 review notes that one family first classed as CLN9 was reclassified as a variant of CLN5 after a CLN5 change was found, and that another person proposed as CLN9 has a CLN2 change (CNS Neuroscience and Therapeutics, 2025). Orphanet now marks CLN9 disease as obsolete (ORPHA:228357).

Where UK families can get help

Your clinical team comes first for diagnosis, care and any questions about studies. These services can also help:

  • Batten Disease Family Association (BDFA): the UK charity for families affected by Batten disease, now registered as the Batten Disease Family Association CIO, charity 1205650.
  • Evelina London Batten disease service: a specialist service at St Thomas' Hospital, London, which takes referrals on a shared care basis so that local teams keep caring for a child with expert input.
  • Great Ormond Street Hospital, metabolic medicine: GOSH's enzyme laboratory also offers NHS blood tests for the CLN1 and CLN2 enzymes.
  • NIHR Be Part of Research: the National Institute for Health and Care Research service where you can search UK studies and register your interest.
  • If you are worried about symptoms now, contact your GP or NHS 111.
  • Newly diagnosed and FAQ: other organisations and public information
  • Contact Anisia's Hope at info@fightingbattendisease.org
