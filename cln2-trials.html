Past studies and what they found

Treatment studies that have finished, stopped or never started, with what was reported. Small, uncontrolled studies cannot show whether a treatment works. We quote the authors' own conclusions and link to the source.

  • Gene therapy with AAV2 (AAV2CUhCLN2) (2004 to 2019). Sponsor: Weill Cornell Medical College. Registry: NCT00151216.
    Ten children received a gene therapy placed at 12 sites in the brain through surgery.
    What was published: One child died 49 days after surgery after prolonged seizures, and the cause of some serious adverse events could not be determined. The rate of decline on the neurological rating scale was slower than in untreated children, but the study was small, not randomised and had no contemporaneous control group (PMID 18473686, Human Gene Therapy, 2008). A 2025 follow up found that three of the ten lived much longer than the others; genotype did not explain the difference (PMID 39745261).
  • Gene therapy with AAVrh.10 (AAVrh.10hCLN2) (2010 to 2020). Sponsor: Weill Cornell Medical College. Registry: NCT01161576, NCT01414985.
    A second generation gene therapy given through six openings in the skull to 12 brain sites, without immune suppression.
    What was published: Enzyme levels in spinal fluid rose 1.3 to 2.6 fold. Eight children with mild to moderate disease formed the main treated group. The rate of decline in combined motor and language scores was 42.4% slower than in an untreated Weill Cornell natural history group and 47.5% slower than in an untreated European natural history group. Adverse events were those expected and none caused long term disability. The authors concluded that further improvement is needed to halt progression (PMID 33268510, Science Translational Medicine, 2020).
  • Neural stem cell transplant (HuCNS-SC) (2006 to 2011). Sponsor: StemCells, Inc.. Registry: NCT00337636, NCT01238315.
    A phase 1 study placed donor neural stem cells into the brains of six children with infantile or late infantile NCL. A follow on study was withdrawn before anyone took part; the registry reason is lack of timely patient accrual.
    What was published: The published report found the transplants and immune suppression were well tolerated, with no adverse events attributed to the donor cells. Because the children had advanced disease, the authors could draw only limited conclusions about benefit (PMID 23581634, Journal of Neurosurgery: Pediatrics, 2013).
  • Cerliponase alfa (BMN 190, Brineura) phase 1/2 and extension (2013 to 2020). Sponsor: BioMarin Pharmaceutical. Registry: NCT01907087, NCT02485899.
    Enzyme replacement given into the fluid spaces of the brain every two weeks. These studies led to approval in the US and Europe in 2017.
    What was published: In 24 children, decline in motor and language scores was less than in 42 untreated children from a natural history group (PMID 29688815, New England Journal of Medicine, 2018). In the extension, 23 children were treated for an average of 272 weeks; 17 completed and 6 stopped. The comparison was again with historical, not concurrent, controls (PMID 38101904, Lancet Neurology, 2024).
  • Cerliponase alfa in children under 3 (2016 to 2022). Sponsor: BioMarin Pharmaceutical. Registry: NCT02678689.
    An open label study in 14 younger children, including some treated before symptoms began.
    What was published: Results are posted on the registry: the main measure was the rate of decline in motor and language scores; 12 of 14 children had at least one serious adverse event and there were no deaths (registry results). Published case reports describe children treated before symptoms (PMID 33990214; PMID 38693756).
  • Cord blood transplant in NCL (outside a CLN specific trial) (reported 2025). Sponsor: Duke University. Registry: Not a registered trial.
    A retrospective report of eight children with NCL who received umbilical cord blood transplants at one US centre, four of them within a wider registered study of many conditions (NCT02254863).
    What was published: No deaths were related to the transplant procedure. Two children with CLN2 died during follow up, and three of four children with CLN3 had stable Hamburg scores over the period reported. This was a small case series without controls (PMID 39471475, Stem Cells Translational Medicine, 2025).

How we source listings

On 3 November 2026 we searched ClinicalTrials.gov, the EU Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS), the EU Clinical Trials Register and the ISRCTN registry by condition name, gene name and older names for each form (for example late infantile NCL for CLN2 and juvenile NCL for CLN3). Each entry links to its registry record. We list what the registry says, flag records that have not been updated for more than two years, and note known stops reported by sponsors. We do not repeat company claims about results.

We could not search WHO ICTRP, ANZCTR (Australia and New Zealand), ChiCTR (China) and jRCT (Japan) automatically on 3 November 2026, because the sites blocked automated searches or did not return results. Studies in those regions that are also on ClinicalTrials.gov are included above.

Search tip

You can search public registries yourself, and it is best to discuss anything you find with your clinical team:

  • ClinicalTrials.gov: try “Batten”, “neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis” or “CLN2”.
  • EU CTIS: trials in the European Union since 2023.
  • NIHR Be Part of Research: UK studies, with the option to register your interest.
  • WHO ICTRP: brings together registries from many countries.

This tip is general guidance only. It is not medical advice.
